My research is in **labor and demographic economics**, particularly migration, fertility and family formation, and **urban and regional economics**, with a focus on housing. My current papers combine original historical district data, the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), and local housing-market data to study family transitions in Germany.

## Job Market Paper

### German Internal Migration: A Marriage Market Perspective

After German reunification, divorce rates fell in West German districts with stronger historical ties to the East, while marriage rates did not rise. I digitize and harmonize records for 320 districts from 1985 to 2007, with divorce records extending to 1980 for 130 districts. To predict migration exposure, I combine the share of each district’s 1961 population that had moved from East Germany before the Berlin Wall with subsequent young-adult arrival rates in other West German states. The divorce decline emerged by 1994 and persisted. Comparing the top and bottom quintiles of the historical settlement share, the relative decline averaged 14 percent of the mean divorce rate. Event studies detect no differential trends before reunification. Scaled by gross arrivals measured from 1991 onward, the decline corresponds to 9 percent of the mean per standard deviation of exposure. Fewer divorces without more marriages challenges the prediction that a larger pool of potential partners would increase both. Higher costs of forming and dissolving households could explain this pattern, although the district data cannot directly test that mechanism.

## Working Papers

### Homeownership and Family Expansion: Evidence from Germany

Does homeownership raise fertility? Using the German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP), I instrument own homeownership with parental homeownership among West German women, comparing women within the same years and districts. The evidence is informative at the transition from a first to a second child: the estimates imply that owner-occupation raises the annual probability of a second birth by 15 to 24 percentage points from a baseline of 11 percent, with weak-instrument-robust intervals that exclude zero but are wide. Estimates for first and third births and for the number of children are too imprecise to show or rule out effects. Parental homeownership also provides rent-free and reduced-rent housing, a channel outside own ownership; if this housing affects fertility as ownership does, the effect of ownership is about 10 percentage points.

### Transition to Adulthood: Local Rents and Leaving the Parental Home

Do rising rents near the parental home delay young adults’ first move out? I link German Socio-Economic Panel (SOEP) residence histories to local apartment rents (RWI-GEO-REDX) and compare young adults living in the same postal code in different years. Among those without a migration background, a 10% increase in local rents in the previous year is associated with a 0.7 percentage-point lower probability of leaving home that year. The estimate is imprecise, and I find no clear relationship for first- or second-generation immigrants.

## Research Agenda

### Does giving up a low rent to move to a larger home delay a second birth?

For parents with a spare room, a favorable lease can make a second child easier to afford. Parents who need more space may have to pay the current market rent on the whole flat when they move. I plan to study how this trade-off shapes when renters in Germany have a second child and whether they move or raise both children in their current home.

### Do Germany’s benefit rules for young adults under 25 affect when they leave the parental home?

Young adults receiving income support may need prior approval for rent to be covered after a move. Staying at home can also reduce their entitlement when their parents’ income is counted. I plan to examine how these two rules together affect whether young adults live with a parent and how the response varies with family resources.

### Does affordable housing near employers help more school leavers start an apprenticeship?

School leavers can often commute to a nearby firm from the parental home. Accepting an offer farther away may mean paying rent on an apprentice’s wage. I plan to study whether access to affordable accommodation changes where young people train and whether it enables those who would otherwise miss out to start and complete an apprenticeship.

### Did COVID-19 restrictions increase support for the far right in Germany and the United States?

School closures disrupted work and childcare and may have drawn voters toward candidates who opposed them. Opposition to restrictions could also reflect political preferences that predated the pandemic. I plan to study whether longer closures changed turnout or support for the AfD in Germany and for far-right candidates in US elections, taking local infection trends into account. I am particularly interested in whether any electoral response persisted after schools reopened.

Research ideas to revisit

1. Does building more family-sized housing change how many children existing residents have? A city can record more births after larger apartments are built because residents have another child, because families expecting a child move in, or because growing families stay instead of leaving. I would follow people who lived in the area before construction, including those who later move away. This would distinguish a policy that changes family size from one that changes where families raise their children.

2. Does giving up a below-market lease delay a second birth for renters who need another room? For parents with a spare room, low rent can make a second child easier to afford. For parents in a smaller flat, moving means paying current market rents on the space they already occupy as well as on the extra room. I would study whether these families postpone a second birth, move before having another child, or raise two children in their existing home.

3. Does access to affordable housing help school leavers start apprenticeships they would otherwise forgo? Two firms can offer the same training wage, yet only one offer may be affordable if a young person can commute to it from the parental home. I would examine whether access to accommodation before applicants choose a placement changes who enters and completes training. The policy payoff depends on how many additional young people train and how many simply choose a different employer or town.

4. Does delayed access to a first mortgage widen the wealth gap between migrant and native-born households? Households with similar earnings can enter homeownership years apart if one can draw on family wealth for a deposit and the other must save from wages. Buying earlier exposes a household to leveraged gains when prices rise and to larger losses when they fall. I would study how a change in mortgage access affects the subsequent net wealth of first-time buyers and whether its effects differ by migration background and parental resources.

5. Do brief childcare closures leave lasting gaps in mothers’ and fathers’ earnings after care resumes? When a nursery closes unexpectedly, one parent may reduce hours or give up a shift to cover care. If that interruption costs a promotion, prompts a job change, or alters how the couple divides paid work, its effects can outlast the closure itself. I would follow both parents after reopening to determine when a temporary loss of care becomes a persistent career cost.
